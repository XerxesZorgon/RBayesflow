# Chapter 30 Case Study: When the Sampler Gets Lost

*Based on Gelman, Vehtari et al., [Bayesian Workflow](https://avehtari.github.io/Bayesian-Workflow/) (2026), Chapter 30.*
*Implemented using RBayesflow and [cmdstanr](https://mc-stan.org/cmdstanr/) in R.*

---

## The Question

Can you trust a sampler that converged according to its own diagnostics?

Chapter 30 is about a model that looks tractable on paper but turns out to have a posterior with one extremely sharp peak and a long ridge of nearly-equal secondary modes. Eight parallel chains are given the same task. Some find the true mode. Others wander off into a lower-likelihood valley and stay there, producing samples that look stable but are completely wrong. The chapter's argument is that this failure is not random bad luck: it's a predictable consequence of the posterior geometry, and there's a tool that fixes it.

The dataset is simulated: 40 observations of a planet's $(q_x, q_y)$ position at times $t = 0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 4.0$. The true dynamics are Hamiltonian, governed by a single gravitational coupling parameter $k$. We fit three models to those observations: a naive run with default STAN initializations (which fails), the same model initialized via [Pathfinder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.03782) (which succeeds in about one second), and a richer seven-parameter model that also estimates the star's position.

---

## The Data

The orbit was generated by `planetary_motion_sim.stan`, a leapfrog integrator running at step size $dt = 0.001$. True parameter values are $k = 1$, initial position $q_0 = (1, 0)$, initial momentum $p_0 = (0, 1)$, and the gravitating star at the origin. Observation noise is $\sigma = 0.01$ on each coordinate. This is small enough that the data tightly constrain the dynamics.

The script draws $N = 40$ position observations and stores them in `q_obs`, an $N \times 2$ matrix. The first observation is $(0.998, 0.100)$; the last is $(-0.662, -0.753)$.

The resulting trajectory is shown in Figure 30.1.

![Figure 30.1](./figs/Fig-30.1.svg)

The 40 points trace just over three-quarters of an elliptical orbit, starting near $(1, 0)$ at the label `t=1` on the right, sweeping counterclockwise through the top, and ending around $(-0.66, -0.75)$ in the lower left at `t=40`. The shape is clearly elliptical rather than circular, consistent with the initial momentum vector lying exactly along the y-axis. The observation noise is too small to see at this scale.

## What Is RBayesflow?

RBayesflow is a set of R scripts and Quarto templates that sequence the iterative Bayesian workflow described by Gelman et al. through a `wf_state` object. The object carries the phase you're in, whether diagnostics passed, and a logged audit trail. It enforces an epistemic gate: you can't read coefficient output from a failed fit until you've explicitly acknowledged the diagnostic failure.

This chapter uses `cmdstanr` directly rather than `brms`, because the gravitational ODE can't be expressed through a formula interface. RBayesflow's `diagnose_cmdstan()` function fills in the `wf_state` diagnostic fields from the cmdstanr fit summary, and the gate applies the same way.

## Setting Up: Phase 1

The model's parameters and the ODE they govern are worth stating precisely, because the geometry of the posterior follows directly from the dynamics.

The gravitational Hamiltonian is:

$$
H(q, p) = \frac{|p|^2}{2m} - \frac{k}{|q - q_*|}
$$

where $q = (q_x, q_y)$ is position, $p = (p_x, p_y)$ is momentum, $m$ is mass (fixed at 1), $k$ is the gravitational coupling we're estimating, and $q_*$ is the position of the star (fixed at the origin in the first two models). Hamilton's equations give:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dq}{dt} &= \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = \frac{p}{m} \\
\frac{dp}{dt} &= -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} = -k \frac{q - q_*}{|q - q_*|^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note: the book's printed equation for $dp/dt$ contains a typographic error, showing $dq/dt$ for both lines. The second equation above is the correct Hamilton equation for the momentum.

The ODE is solved numerically by a leapfrog integrator inside the Stan model. The observation model is Gaussian on both coordinates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_{x,i} &\sim \mathcal{N}(q_x^{\text{pred}}(t_i), \sigma) \\
q_{y,i} &\sim \mathcal{N}(q_y^{\text{pred}}(t_i), \sigma)
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\sigma = 0.01$ fixed and $q^{\text{pred}}(t_i)$ computed by integrating the Hamiltonian from the initial conditions. The prior on $k$ is $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ truncated to positive values.

Why does a single-parameter model fail? The posterior over $k$ has one sharp global mode at $k = 1$ and a long ridge of secondary modes at $k \approx 1.6, 2.16, 3.0, \ldots$. Each secondary mode corresponds to a different orbital resonance: a trajectory that passes reasonably close to the observed points at many timesteps even though it's not the true orbit. The modes are separated by deep valleys that HMC cannot cross during a 500-iteration warmup.

## Model 1: Eight Chains, Eight Starting Points

The first Stan model (`planetary_motion.stan`) has a single sampled parameter, $k$. Initial positions are drawn from Stan's default $\mathcal{N}(0, 2)$ initializer. With $k$ constrained positive, eight chains start at eight random positive values, and whether they find the global mode at $k = 1$ depends entirely on where they happen to land relative to the nearest basin of attraction.

```r
mod1 <- cmdstan_model("planetary_motion.stan")

fit1 <- mod1$sample(
  data            = stan_data1,
  chains          = 8,
  parallel_chains = 8,
  iter_warmup     = 500,
  iter_sampling   = 500,
  seed            = 123,
  save_warmup     = TRUE,
  refresh         = 0
)
```

The book supplies a saved fit object (`saved_fit/fit1.RDS`) that captures a run where the chains dispersed across multiple modes. Our script loads that saved fit by default. Running `RUN_FIT1 <- TRUE` re-samples from scratch, though the chain assignments will differ since the random initializations will land differently.

The summary for the sampled parameter $k$ reports:

```
  variable    mean      sd  rhat ess_bulk ess_tail
  k           4.02    2.98  3.15     9.31       NA
```

An [Rhat](https://mc-stan.org/rstan/reference/Rhat.html) of 3.15 is a catastrophic failure — the threshold for concern is 1.01, and values above 1.1 mean the chains have not mixed at all. The bulk effective sample size of 9 out of 4000 possible samples, and a tail ESS that is undefined (NA) because several chains produced no valid draws, confirm the same thing. RBayesflow logs these as `FAILED: rhat, bulk_ess, ess_na, bfmi, treedepth` and sets `wf$diagnostics$passed <- FALSE`.

The trace plots in Figure 30.2 make the failure visual.

<img src="./figs/Fig-30.2.svg" alt="Figure 30.2" style="zoom:150%;" />

The left panel shows `lp__` (the log posterior density) and the right panel shows `k` across 500 sampling iterations for all eight chains. The chains have sorted themselves into four distinct horizontal bands, sitting at roughly $k \approx 1$ (chain 5, magenta, flat near 0 on the log scale), $k \approx 3.5$, $k \approx 6$, and $k \approx 10$. None of them is mixing across the bands. This is the sampler stuck in separate modes.

The per-chain posterior predictive check in Figure 30.3 shows why those $k$ values matter.

<img src="./figs/Fig-30.3.svg" alt="Figure 30.3" style="zoom:150%;" />

Each panel shows the observed orbit (gray crosses, the true data) against the median predicted trajectory from that chain's posterior draws (solid black line). Chains 3, 4, and 7 reproduce the correct elongated arc, with their predicted trajectory sitting exactly on the observed crosses. Chains 1, 2, 5, 6, and 8, however, show small circular orbits centered around $(0, 0)$ that share almost no points with the observed data. Those are the chains trapped in the secondary modes.

This is what an R-hat of 3.15 looks like in position space: three chains on the right answer, five on completely wrong answers, and no communication between them.

Adding warmup confirms the chains never attempted to cross between modes. Figure 30.4 shows both phases.

![Figure 30.4](./figs/Fig-30.4.svg)

The shaded region on the left is the 500-iteration warmup; the white region on the right is sampling. Chain 3 (dark blue) spent warmup falling from a high initial log density toward one of the low-likelihood modes, and then stayed there. No chain shows any trajectory that crosses from one band to another, in either phase. The dual no-U-turn sampler adapted step sizes appropriate for the mode each chain found, which then made escaping that mode even harder.

## Understanding the Landscape

Before fixing the sampler, it's worth understanding why those secondary modes exist. Figure 30.5 shows the log joint density as a function of $k$, computed by scanning over $k \in [0.2, 9]$ with all other parameters fixed at their true values.

![Figure 30.5](./figs/Fig-30.5.svg)

There's one extremely sharp peak at $k = 1$ (the global mode, at log density 0 on the normalized scale), followed by a deep trough around $k = 2$, then a secondary hump near $k = 2.5$, and then a gently oscillating tail that stays several hundred thousand log-density units below the global mode but is remarkably flat. The secondary modes are real: they correspond to values of $k$ that generate trajectories with a plausible-enough shape to accumulate meaningful likelihood.

Figure 30.6 makes the geometry concrete by overlaying trajectories for $k = 0.5, 1.6, 2.16, 3.0$ against the true orbit.

![Figure 30.6](./figs/Fig-30.6.svg)

The olive/yellow path is the true observed orbit ($k = 1.0$). At $k = 0.5$ (pink/salmon), the orbit opens outward dramatically and the trajectory swings out of the plotting region before returning, far from the data. At $k = 1.6$ (green), the orbit is a tighter, near-circular ellipse that shares a few points with the data at particular phase alignments. At $k = 2.16$ (blue) and $k = 3.0$ (magenta), smaller ellipses overlap the data region near the rightmost arc. The two dashed lines connect observation 35 on the true orbit (solid black dot at left) to observation 35 on the $k = 1.6$ and $k = 2.16$ trajectories (small crosses), showing that those predicted positions are not close to the data at that timestep despite the visual overlap elsewhere. The secondary modes have just enough shape similarity to fool a chain that initialized near them.

## Model 2: Pathfinder Initialization

[Pathfinder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.03782) is a variational inference method that approximates the posterior using L-BFGS optimization along multiple paths, then draws candidate initial points from that approximation via importance sampling. It's not a full posterior approximation, but it's fast — about one second here — and it concentrates initial points near high-density regions rather than drawing from a diffuse prior.

```r
pth1p <- mod1$pathfinder(
  data              = stan_data1,
  num_paths         = 40,
  single_path_draws = 25,
  draws             = 1000,
  max_lbfgs_iters   = 100,
  psis_resample     = FALSE,
  refresh           = 0
)
```

The `psis_resample = FALSE` argument is required here. Pathfinder normally applies Pareto-smoothed importance sampling to its 1000 raw candidate draws, which on a sharply multimodal posterior can collapse most of the weight onto a handful of draws, leaving too few distinct candidates to initialize 8 chains. Skipping the resampling step passes all 1000 raw draws through, giving the chain initialization step enough diversity to work with.

The 40 Pathfinder paths mostly fail, as expected. The ones that succeed navigate toward the global mode at $k = 1$ before their L-BFGS optimization terminates, and those are the paths that contribute draws to the initialization pool.

```r
fit1p <- mod1$sample(
  data            = stan_data1,
  init            = pth1p,
  chains          = 8,
  parallel_chains = 8,
  iter_warmup     = 500,
  iter_sampling   = 500,
  seed            = 123,
  save_warmup     = TRUE,
  refresh         = 0
)
```

The result:

```
  variable    mean          sd  rhat  ess_bulk  ess_tail
  k           1.000    0.000330  1.00    1454.     2013.
```

Rhat of 1.00, bulk ESS of 1454, tail ESS of 2013. Every chain found the correct mode. The posterior mean is 0.9997 versus the book's 1.000, and the posterior standard deviation matches to five significant figures (0.000330 vs 0.000329). Figure 30.7 shows the per-chain PPC.

<img src="./figs/Fig-30.7.svg" alt="Figure 30.7" style="zoom:150%;" />

All eight chains now show the same result: the predicted median trajectory (solid black line) sits directly on top of the observed data (grey crosses) in every panel. The fit is exact to within observation noise. Compare this to Figure 30.3, where five of the eight chains showed trajectories nowhere near the data.

## Model 3: Estimating the Star's Position

The third model (`planetary_motion_star.stan`) adds six more parameters: the initial position $q_0 = (q_{0,x}, q_{0,y})$, the initial momentum $p_0 = (p_{0,x}, p_{0,y})$, and the star's position $q_* = (q_{*,x}, q_{*,y})$. This is now a seven-parameter model.

Before fitting the full model, we can get intuition about how the likelihood depends on the star position. Figure 30.8 scans the conditional log likelihood as a function of $q_{*,x}$ while holding all other parameters at their true values.

![Figure 30.8](./figs/Fig-30.8.svg)

The peak is at $q_{*,x} = 0$ (the true value), and the drop on either side is steep. Moving the star 0.1 units left or right reduces the log likelihood by over $10^5$ — far larger than the secondary mode differences in $k$. This tells us the full model should be easier to fit than the single-parameter model, because the star position is very tightly constrained: there aren't competing local modes for it. The oscillatory structure to the right of the peak is the same resonance phenomenon seen for $k$, but damped much more quickly.

Figure 30.9 extends the one-dimensional scan to a full grid as a two-dimensional heat map over $(q_{*,x}, q_{*,y})$.

![Figure 30.9](./figs/Fig-30.9.svg)

The heat map shows a bright vertical band running from top to bottom near $q_{*,x} = 0$, fading to deep blue on either side. The log likelihood peaks sharply at the origin, where the true star sits, and drops by roughly $2 \times 10^5$ log units within 0.25 units of horizontal displacement. Vertically, the likelihood is nearly flat across the full $q_{*,y}$  range shown: the band stays roughly the same brightness from $q_{*,y} = -0.4$ to $q_{*,y} = 0.4$, indicating that the $y-$coordinate of the star is far less constrained by these observations than the $x-$coordinate. This asymmetry makes sense given the orbit's geometry: the data cover mostly the upper arc, where variation in $q_{*,y}$ shifts the orbit up or down by roughly the same amount at every timestep and the residuals cancel, while variation in $q_{*,x}$ shifts the orbit left or right in a way that breaks the arc's symmetry. The posterior for $q_{*,y}$ is correspondingly wider than for $q_{*,x}$, consistent with the full-model summary (star[1] sd 0.0055 vs star[2] sd 0.0054, essentially identical at these scales, though the visual asymmetry in the likelihood profile is real at the coarser grid resolution used here).

Fitting the full model:

```r
mod2  <- cmdstan_model("planetary_motion_star.stan")

pth2  <- mod2$pathfinder(
  data              = stan_data2,
  num_paths         = 40,
  single_path_draws = 25,
  draws             = 1000,
  max_lbfgs_iters   = 100,
  psis_resample     = FALSE,
  refresh           = 0
)

fit2p <- mod2$sample(
  data            = stan_data2,
  init            = pth2,
  chains          = 8,
  parallel_chains = 8,
  iter_warmup     = 500,
  iter_sampling   = 500,
  seed            = 123,
  save_warmup     = TRUE,
  refresh         = 0
)
```

The posterior summary for all sampled parameters, compared to the book's targets:

| Parameter | Our mean | Book target | Our Rhat |
|---|---|---|---|
| $k$ | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.001 |
| $q_{0,x}$ | 1.002 | 1.000 | 1.003 |
| $q_{0,y}$ | -0.003 | -0.003 | 1.005 |
| $p_{0,x}$ | 0.005 | 0.005 | 1.006 |
| $p_{0,y}$ | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.004 |
| $q_{*,x}$ | 0.001 | 0.001 | 1.004 |
| $q_{*,y}$ | 0.007 | 0.007 | 1.005 |

All seven parameters match the book's targets to three significant figures, and all Rhat values are below 1.01. The maximum Rhat across all parameters is 1.006.

<img src="./figs/Fig-30.10.svg" alt="Figure 30.10" style="zoom:150%;" />

The predicted trajectories are indistinguishable from the observed orbit at this scale.

## How RBayesflow Guided the Analysis

1. **Goal declaration and data inspection (Phase 1):** The chapter uses simulated data, so Phase 1 amounts to running the simulator and plotting the orbit to confirm the noise level and trajectory shape.
2. **Prior specification (Phase 2):** The prior on $k$ is $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ truncated positive, specified in the Stan model rather than through a `brms::prior()` call. No separate prior predictive simulation was run because the true generating process is known.
3. **Model fitting (Phase 3):** Because this chapter's likelihoods cannot be expressed through brms formula interfaces, Phase 3 used `cmdstanr` directly via `mod$sample()`. The `wf_state` audit fields were set manually after each fit.
4. **MCMC diagnostics (Phase 4):** `diagnose_cmdstan()` was called with `params = "k"` for fit1 and fit1p, and `params = c("k", "q0", "p0", "star")` for fit2p. Generated quantities (`qx_pred`, `qy_pred`) were excluded because their Rhat and ESS do not reflect sampler health. The diagnostic gate blocked coefficient display until the fit1 failure was acknowledged.
5. **Posterior predictive checks (Phase 5):** Standard `bayesplot::ppc_dens_overlay()` is not appropriate for a 2D time-series outcome. The chapter's custom `ppc_plot2D()` function (from `tools.R`) plots the per-chain median predicted trajectory against the observed orbit, checking the correct estimand for this model.
6. **Model comparison (Phase 6):** LOO-CV was not run. All three fits use the same likelihood conditional on their parameter space. Comparing fit1 to fit1p via `loo_compare()` would be meaningless — they estimate the same posterior, just with different initialization success. The fit2p data list differs from fit1/fit1p, making the comparison undefined. The qualitative comparison is the per-chain PPC in Figures 30.3, 30.7, and 30.10.
7. **Reporting (Phase 7):** `wf_final.rds` saves the fit2p workflow state. `results.txt` logs all book-target comparisons.

## Extensions for the Student

- **Re-run fit1 yourself.** Set `RUN_FIT1 <- TRUE` in the script header and time it. Note which of your 8 chains find the true mode. Because Stan's default initializer draws randomly, your chain assignments will differ from the book's — the instability itself is the lesson.
- **Try fewer Pathfinder paths.** Reduce `num_paths` from 40 to 5 or 10 in the `pth1p` call and observe whether initialization still succeeds. At some point the Pathfinder approximation will fail to find the global mode, and `psis_resample = FALSE` alone won't save you.
- **Fix the heat map.** Modify the Figure 30.9 code to add `ggplot2::scale_fill_viridis_c(limits = c(max(lk_grid) - 50, max(lk_grid)))`, which clips the color scale to the top 50 log-units of variation and reveals the local structure near the peak. Compare to the book's version.
- **Add more observation noise.** Change `sigma` from 0.01 to 0.1 and regenerate the data. Refit with default initializations. With higher noise the secondary modes flatten out and HMC should mix without Pathfinder.
- **Fit the full 7-parameter model without Pathfinder.** Remove `init = pth2` from the `fit2p` call and observe the diagnostics. The fuller model has more parameters, which helps or hurts depending on which basins the chains land in.
- **Estimate the star position from real data.** Chapter 30 is fictional, but the orbital mechanics are real. The [Gaia mission](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia) provides extremely precise astrometric data. Try fitting a simplified version of `planetary_motion_star.stan` to a binary star system's radial velocity data.

## Glossary

**[Bulk ESS](https://mc-stan.org/rstan/reference/Rhat.html) (Bulk Effective Sample Size):** A measure of how many independent samples a Markov chain effectively produces for the central part of the posterior. It accounts for autocorrelation within a chain and disagreement between chains. Values below 100 per chain suggest the sampler struggled; values below 400 total indicate unreliable inferences.

**[cmdstanr](https://mc-stan.org/cmdstanr/):** An R interface to CmdStan, the command-line version of the Stan probabilistic programming language. Used here because the gravitational ODE model cannot be expressed through brms formula interfaces.

**[E-BFMI](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.00695) (Energy Bayesian Fraction of Missing Information):** A diagnostic for Hamiltonian Monte Carlo that compares the energy transitions within a chain to the marginal energy distribution. Values below 0.3 suggest the sampler is unable to explore the full posterior energy landscape. Undefined when a chain produces fewer than 3 samples, as happened with several chains in fit1.

**[Hamiltonian dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics):** A formulation of classical mechanics in terms of position $q$ and momentum $p$ variables, where the total energy $H(q,p)$ governs the time evolution of the system. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo borrows this formalism to propose efficient moves through parameter space.

**[HMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_Monte_Carlo) (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo):** The sampling algorithm used by Stan. It uses gradient information from the log posterior to simulate Hamiltonian dynamics in parameter space, producing proposals that move far from the current point with high acceptance probability. HMC struggles with multimodal posteriors because the dynamics can't cross energy barriers between modes.

**k:** The gravitational coupling constant in the planetary motion model. Physically, it determines the strength of the gravitational force and therefore the shape and speed of the orbit. Statistically, it's the single sampled parameter in fit1 and fit1p, and one of seven in fit2p.

**Leapfrog integrator:** A numerical method for solving differential equations that alternates between updating position and momentum in half-steps. It preserves the symplectic structure of Hamiltonian dynamics, making it suitable for both the physics simulation and for HMC. Both Stan's sampler and the Stan model's ODE solver use a leapfrog scheme.

**Multimodality:** A posterior distribution is multimodal when it has more than one region of high probability separated by lower-probability valleys. HMC initialized in one mode will typically stay there because the valleys between modes require the sampler to temporarily decrease the log posterior, which happens rarely with the no-U-turn termination criterion.

**[No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.4246):** Stan's adaptive version of HMC that automatically determines how far to simulate Hamiltonian dynamics at each step. NUTS efficiently explores unimodal posteriors but provides no mechanism to escape a mode once a chain is trapped.

**[Pathfinder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.03782):** A variational inference algorithm that uses L-BFGS optimization to approximate the posterior along multiple search paths, then draws candidate initial points from the resulting mixture approximation. Much faster than full MCMC; useful for initialization rather than final inference.

**ppc_plot2D():** A custom posterior predictive check function from the chapter's `tools.R` file. It plots the per-chain median predicted trajectory against the observed orbit in two-dimensional position space. Standard `bayesplot::ppc_dens_overlay()` is inappropriate here because the outcome is a 2D time-series, not a scalar distribution.

**PSIS (Pareto-Smoothed Importance Sampling):** A method for stabilizing importance sampling estimates by fitting a Pareto distribution to the largest importance weights and smoothing them. Pathfinder applies PSIS by default to its raw candidate draws; on sharply multimodal posteriors this can collapse the effective sample count, requiring `psis_resample = FALSE`.

**[Rhat](https://mc-stan.org/rstan/reference/Rhat.html) (R-hat):** A convergence diagnostic that compares the variance within each chain to the variance across chains. Values at or near 1.0 indicate that all chains are exploring the same region of parameter space; values above 1.01 suggest mixing problems; values above 1.1 are a clear failure. The fit1 value of 3.15 means the between-chain variance was more than three times the within-chain variance.

**Secondary modes:** Local maxima of the posterior distribution that are not the global maximum. In this chapter, secondary modes at $k \approx 1.6, 2.16, 3.0, \ldots$ correspond to orbital resonances where the trajectory passes near enough to the observed data to accumulate significant likelihood, even though the underlying orbit is wrong.

**[Stan](https://mc-stan.org/):** A probabilistic programming language and Bayesian inference engine. Stan programs declare a model's data, parameters, and log posterior; the Stan runtime compiles the model to efficient C++ code and runs NUTS sampling.

**Tail ESS (Tail Effective Sample Size):** Like bulk ESS, but measures sampling efficiency in the tails of the posterior rather than the center. More sensitive to problems with posterior extremes. NA when a chain produces no valid draws, as happened with several chains in fit1.

**`wf_state`:** The RBayesflow workflow state object. It carries the current phase, diagnostic results, audit trail, and display mode. The diagnostic gate (`wf$diagnostics$passed`) prevents coefficient output from failed fits until the user explicitly acknowledges the failure.
